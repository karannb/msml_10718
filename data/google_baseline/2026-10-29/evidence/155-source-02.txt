- paragraph: Your plant is etiolated, which means it's stretching to reach light it isn't getting. The stretched stem, widely spaced leaves, and rosette that has opened out and lost its tightness are all signs of light starvation. Once a succulent grows this way, the stretching is permanent. You cannot make those internodes tighten back up.
- paragraph: The good news is that you can behead the top to reshape the plant, and both the beheaded rosette and the stump will regrow in better form once they have proper light.
- heading "How do I behead and propagate a stretched succulent?" [level=2]
- paragraph: Cut off the top rosette and strip the lowest leaves from the stem you cut. Let the cut surface dry and callus over for several days in a warm, dry spot. Don't rush this step. Set the beheaded top into dry succulent mix in a pot with a drainage hole, and leave it alone. After several days, test for roots by giving the base a very gentle tug. If you feel resistance, roots have formed. If it pulls free, wait longer.
- paragraph: Once the rooted top is established, you can begin to water it normally.

- heading "How often should I water a stretched succulent, and why can't I just give it more water?" [level=2]
- paragraph: "This is where the common advice goes wrong. The temptation is to give the plant more sun and more water at once, but doing that kills these plants. Here's why: a succulent in poor light uses very little water, and the soil stays wet longer. If you water it more before you've fixed the light, the soil stays even wetter, and the combination of darkness and wet soil accelerates the stretching and invites rot at the base."
- paragraph: "Fix the light first. Once the plant has adequate light, water it normally for a succulent: give it a thorough soak until water runs freely from the drainage hole, then let the mix dry out completely before you water again. There's no fixed schedule. Frequency is a response to the mix being dry. Don't water on a calendar."

- paragraph: A standard potting mix holds far too much water around succulent roots. Use a gritty, fast-draining cactus and succulent mix, and make sure your pot has a drainage hole.
- heading "Where should I place my succulent?" [level=2]
- paragraph: Move it into good light, but do it gradually. A plant that has been grown in dim light will scorch if you put it directly into a bright window or full sun. Take it to its brighter location in stages over a couple of weeks, and it will harden off without leaf damage.
- heading "What about the bottom leaves that are shriveling?" [level=2]
- paragraph: A healthy rosette continuously consumes and sheds its oldest, lowest leaves as it grows. Dry, papery lower leaves that pull away cleanly are completely normal. Don't be alarmed by them.
- paragraph: What is not normal is leaves that are soft, translucent, or yellowing, or shriveling that spreads upward into the newer growth. That pattern suggests a root problem from too much water or poor drainage. It's worth paying attention to.

- heading "Can I identify what kind of succulent this is?" [level=2]
- paragraph: A badly stretched rosette is very hard to identify with confidence because etiolation changes the spacing of the leaves, their shape, and the color that identification depends on. The care is the same across all the common rosette succulents regardless of which species yours actually is, so you don't need the name to fix the plant right now.
- paragraph: Once your succulent has grown in good light for a season, it will be far easier to identify accurately, and if you want to know then, you can ask with a photo of the new growth.
