  - heading [level=1]: How to Help a Struggling Houseplant Recover
  - paragraph:
    - text: Has your favorite
    - link:
      - /url: /az/plants/houseplants/
      - text: houseplant
    - text: started looking a little worse for wear?
    - link:
      - /url: /az/inspire/blog/reasons-plants-turn-yellow/
      - text: Yellow leaves
    - text: ", drooping stems, brown tips, stalled growth, or leaf drop can all be signs that something isn't quite right."
  - paragraph: The good news is that these symptoms don't necessarily mean your plant is beyond saving. Many common houseplant problems can be corrected once you identify the cause. With a few simple adjustments—and a little patience—it's often possible to help a struggling houseplant recover and start growing again.
  - paragraph: Use this guide to diagnose common houseplant issues and learn how to help your plant get back on track.
  - separator
  - heading [level=2]: What Is Your Plant Trying to Tell You?
  - paragraph: Your plant's symptoms can provide important clues about what's wrong.

  - paragraph:
    - strong: "Quick Tip:"
    - text: Houseplants often show similar symptoms for different reasons. Before making multiple changes at once, try identifying the most likely cause and addressing it first.
  - paragraph: Not sure where to begin? Work through this simple checklist, starting with the most common causes first. Giving your plant time to respond between changes can help you determine what's helping it recover.
  - heading [level=3]: Houseplant Recovery Checklist
  - paragraph: ☐ 1. Check soil moisture ☐ 2. Assess lighting conditions ☐ 3. Inspect for pests ☐ 4. Check for rootbound roots or poor drainage ☐ 5. Repot if needed ☐ 6. Fertilize after new growth appears
  - paragraph: Give your plant time to respond between changes, and watch for signs of healthy new growth. With a little patience, you'll be able to identify the issue and help your plant get back on track.

  - heading [level=2]:
    - text: Water Properly
  - paragraph: Watering issues are one of the most common reasons houseplants struggle. Both overwatering and underwatering can cause leaves to wilt, yellow, or drop.
  - paragraph: A good rule of thumb is to water less frequently but more thoroughly. Slowly water the soil until excess water drains from the bottom of the pot. This encourages deeper root growth and ensures moisture reaches the entire root system.
  - paragraph: Before watering again, check the soil moisture. Many houseplants prefer the top inch or two of soil to dry slightly between waterings, while others like consistently moist conditions. Moisture meters are a great tool to use to determine soil dryness.
  - paragraph: The type of container matters, too. Terracotta pots dry out more quickly than plastic or glazed ceramic containers, so plants in clay pots often need water more frequently.
  - separator
  - heading [level=2]:
    - text: Make Sure Your Plant Is Getting Enough Light
  - paragraph: Even a well-watered plant can decline if it isn't receiving the right amount of light.
  - paragraph:
    - text: Signs of insufficient light include stretched stems, smaller leaves, faded foliage, and slow growth. If your plant seems sparse or leggy, try moving it closer to a bright window or supplementing with
    - link:
      - /url: /az/products/accessories/for-houseplants/mossify/
      - text: a grow light
    - text: .
  - paragraph:
    - text: Different plants have different
    - link:
      - /url: /az/inspire/blog/year-round-houseplant-care/
      - text: light requirements
    - text: ", so be sure to check the needs of your specific variety. Many popular houseplants prefer bright, indirect light, while others tolerate lower-light conditions."
  - separator
  - heading [level=2]:
    - text: Give the Leaves a Good Cleaning
  - paragraph: Dust naturally accumulates on indoor plant leaves over time. A layer of dust can block sunlight and make it harder for plants to photosynthesize efficiently.
  - paragraph: Wipe leaves gently with a soft, damp cloth or rinse them with lukewarm water. For larger plants, this simple cleaning can make a noticeable difference in appearance and overall health.
  - paragraph: Regular leaf cleaning also gives you an opportunity to inspect for pests before they become a bigger problem.
  - separator
  - heading [level=2]: Check for Pests
  - paragraph:
    - text: Some of the most common
    - link:
      - /url: /az/inspire/blog/defeating-houseplant-pests/
      - text: houseplant pests
    - text: are small enough to go unnoticed until damage becomes severe.
  - heading [level=3]:
    - text: Scale
  - paragraph: Scale insects appear as small brown bumps attached to stems and leaves. They feed on plant sap and can weaken plants over time. Remove them by hand when possible and treat affected plants with insecticidal soap or horticultural oil.
  - heading [level=3]: Spider Mites
  - paragraph: Spider mites are tiny pests that often leave behind fine webbing on leaves and stems. Infested plants may develop speckled, yellowing foliage.
  - paragraph: Because spider mites prefer dry conditions, increasing humidity and isolating affected plants can help slow their spread.
  - heading [level=3]:
    - text: Mealybugs
  - paragraph: Mealybugs look like small clusters of white cotton on stems and leaf joints. Spot-treat them with a cotton swab dipped in isopropyl alcohol, or use insecticidal soap for larger infestations.
  - heading [level=3]: Aphids
  - paragraph: Aphids are soft-bodied insects that feed on new growth and leave behind a sticky residue called honeydew. Small infestations can often be removed with a gentle spray of water or insecticidal soap.
  - separator
  - heading [level=2]:
    - text: Check for Rootbound Conditions
  - paragraph:
    - text: If your plant dries out unusually quickly, stops growing, or has roots emerging from drainage holes, it may have outgrown its
    - link:
      - /url: /az/products/accessories/planters/indoor/
      - text: container
    - text: .
  - paragraph:
    - text: When
    - link:
      - /url: /az/inspire/blog/garden-prep-healthy-roots/
      - text: roots
    - text: become crowded, they have less access to water, nutrients, and oxygen. Moving the plant into a slightly larger pot can give the root system room to expand and improve overall health.
  - paragraph: Choose a new container that is 1–2 inches wider than the current pot. Going too large can cause soil to stay wet longer than the plant needs.
  - separator
  - heading [level=2]:
    - text: Repot if Necessary
  - paragraph:
    - text: Sometimes the issue isn't just the size of the pot—it's
    - link:
      - /url: /az/products/soils-etc/soils/
      - text: the soil
    - text: .
  - paragraph:
    - text: Old potting mix breaks down over time, reducing drainage and limiting airflow around the roots.
    - link:
      - /url: /az/inspire/blog/tips-houseplant-repotting-fertilizing/
      - text: Repotting
    - text: into fresh soil can improve growing conditions and give your plant a fresh start.
  - paragraph:
    - text: Select a potting mix that matches your plant's needs. Tropical houseplants typically appreciate a well-draining indoor potting mix, while succulents and cacti perform best in specialized cactus and succulent blends. For everyday houseplant varieties, we recommend
    - link:
      - /url: https://www.summerwindsnursery.com/az/products/soils-etc/soils/#sw_potting_soil
      - text: SummerWinds Nursery Organic Potting Soil
    - text: .
  - paragraph: When repotting, handle roots gently and remove any dead or mushy sections. Water thoroughly after repotting and allow the plant time to adjust to its new environment.
  - separator
  - heading [level=2]:
    - text: Hold Off on Fertilizer Until Recovery Begins
  - paragraph: It may be tempting to fertilize a struggling plant, but fertilizer isn't a cure-all.
  - paragraph: If a plant is stressed by overwatering, pests, poor light, or root damage, adding fertilizer can sometimes create additional stress. Focus first on correcting the underlying issue.
  - paragraph:
    - text: Once you begin to see healthy new growth, you can resume
    - link:
      - /url: https://www.summerwindsnursery.com/az/inspire/blog/fertilizing-container-gardens/#specialty_fertilizers
      - text: fertilizing
    - text: according to the plant's needs. A balanced houseplant fertilizer or slow-release fertilizer is suitable for many indoor plants during the growing season.
  - separator
  - heading [level=2]:
    - text: Be Patient and Watch for Signs of Recovery
  - paragraph: Reviving a struggling houseplant often takes time. New leaves, improved color, firmer stems, and fresh growth are all signs that your plant is recovering.
  - paragraph: Start by identifying the most likely cause of the problem, make one change at a time, and give your plant a chance to respond. With a little patience and proper care, many houseplants can bounce back surprisingly well.
