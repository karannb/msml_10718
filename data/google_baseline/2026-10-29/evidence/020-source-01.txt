    - 'heading "Cause 1: not enough light (the fix takes 10 minutes)" [level=2]'
    - paragraph:
      - text: Light is the first thing to check, because it explains most stalled chilli plants reported in Estonia and across Northern Europe. A chilli needs 12 to 14 hours of light a day to grow at a normal pace, and a windowsill in February gives about 8 hours of weak light at best.
      - link "University of Minnesota Extension":
        - /url: https://extension.umn.edu/planting-and-growing-guides/lighting-indoor-plants
      - text: notes that plants in low light grow more slowly and use less water, which is why a dark spot leads straight to the overwatering problem in cause 3.
    - paragraph:
      - text: Moving a stalled plant under an LED panel at around 30 cm distance, on a 14-hour timer, typically produces visible new growth within 7 to 10 days. If you only fix one thing, fix this one. A 100 W full-spectrum panel runs at roughly 0,10 € a day in Estonia at 14 hours, and our
      - link "free indoor gardening tools":
        - /url: /free-indoor-gardening-tools/
        - emphasis: free indoor gardening tools
      - text: help you work out light and nutrient settings for your setup.
    - 'heading "Cause 2: nights below 15 °C" [level=2]'
    - paragraph:
      - text: Cold is the second most common reason, and the sneakiest, because the plant does not look damaged. The
      - link "RHS":
        - /url: https://www.rhs.org.uk/vegetables/chilli-pepper/grow-your-own
      - text: says chillies cope with 12 °C at night but grow better above 15 °C, and growth commonly pauses around 13 °C. A windowsill above a radiator that is switched off at night can drop 6 to 8 °C below the room temperature.
    - paragraph: "Taping a thermometer to the sill for a few nights is the easiest way to check: readings near 12 °C explain a stalled plant. Moving the pot about 40 cm into the room, off the sill, is usually enough to bring night temperatures into the high teens, and growth commonly restarts within a week. That is the 10-minute fix. If you cannot move the plant, a heated seed starter base keeps the root zone at 20 to 25 °C regardless of the window."
    - figure:
      - img "Indoor chilli plant on a windowsill, used to illustrate stalled growth from cold temperatures"
    - 'heading "Cause 3: overwatering and wet roots" [level=2]'
    - paragraph: Overwatering means the compost never dries between waterings, so the roots sit without oxygen. When a chilli stops growing, most people water more, which makes it worse. In low light a chilli uses maybe a third of the water it uses under a grow light, so the same watering routine that worked in June drowns the plant in November.
    - paragraph: "Pushing a finger 3 cm into the compost is the simplest check: water only when it comes out dry. For a stalled plant, that often means stretching the interval from every 2 days to every 6. Check that the pot has drainage holes and no standing water in the saucer. Root recovery is slower than the light fix — expect 2 to 3 weeks before new growth appears. A hydroponic system avoids this problem altogether, since the reservoir keeps the root zone at a constant level."
    - 'heading "Cause 4: the pot is too small or too large" [level=2]'
    - paragraph: "Pot size affects growth in both directions. A chilli in a 7 cm pot for more than 4 weeks becomes root-bound and stops. A 5 cm seedling moved straight into a 5 L pot sits in cold, wet compost for weeks and also stops. The RHS sequence works: seed tray, then a 9 cm pot, then a 13 cm pot when roots show at the bottom, then a final 22 cm pot (about 5 L)."
    - paragraph: Tipping a stalled plant out of its pot often shows roots circling the base — a clear sign it is time to move up one size. Growers typically see new growth within about 8 days of repotting from a 9 cm to a 13 cm pot. Do not skip sizes. Twice the diameter is roughly four times the volume, and that is too much wet compost for a small root ball.
    - figure:
      - img "Young chilli seedlings growing in the pods of a LetPot smart garden"
    - 'heading "Cause 5: feeding at the wrong strength" [level=2]'
    - paragraph: Feeding is a fix only when the other four conditions are already right. A chilli in dim, cold, wet compost does not need more fertiliser, it needs less water and more light. But a plant that has been in the same compost for 8 weeks or longer, with pale lower leaves and good light, is usually hungry. Fresh compost feeds a chilli for about 6 weeks, after that you feed weekly at half strength until flowering, then full strength.
    - paragraph:
      - text: "In a hydroponic system, checking EC with a meter is the fastest way to confirm this: a reading near 0,6 mS/cm after a plain-water top-up is well below the 1,8 to 2,2 mS/cm chillies want in the vegetative stage. Adding nutrients to bring the solution back into that range typically shows a visible growth response within a week. If you are not sure what EC means, our guide to"

    - 'heading "Cause 6: transplant shock" [level=2]'
    - paragraph: Transplant shock is a growth pause of 7 to 14 days after repotting, moving the plant to a new room, or a sudden jump in light. It looks alarming but needs no fix except patience. The mistake is to react with extra water or feed, which turns a pause into cause 3 or cause 5.
    - paragraph: After repotting, keeping the plant at the same light distance and temperature, and watering only when the top 3 cm is dry, usually lets growth resume within about 9 to 10 days. If you move a chilli from a window to a grow light, start the lamp at 40 cm and lower it 5 cm every 3 days to avoid scorched leaves.
    - 'heading "Cause 7: a slow variety, or the wrong season" [level=2]'
    - paragraph: Some chillies are simply slow. Habanero types and other Capsicum chinense can take 120 to 150 days from sowing to first ripe fruit, while a cayenne type such as Ardito H is ready in around 90 days. Growth also slows naturally from October to February at our latitude even under good conditions, and that is normal rather than a problem. If you sowed in autumn without a grow light, the plant is waiting for spring.
    - paragraph:
      - text: A cayenne-type hybrid rated 30 000 to 50 000 SHU is a reasonable choice here, since it stays under 60 cm and fruits early. If your plant is a large-fruited or superhot variety, give it a 100 W light and 4 months rather than expecting results in 6 weeks. Our

    - heading "Frequently asked questions" [level=2]
    - heading "How long does a chilli plant take to start growing again after a fix?" [level=3]
    - paragraph: Light fixes typically show new leaves in 7 to 9 days, temperature fixes in about a week, and watering fixes in 2 to 3 weeks, since the roots need time to recover first.
    - heading "How many hours of light does an indoor chilli need?" [level=3]
    - paragraph: 12 to 14 hours a day. Below 8 hours the plant will hold but not grow. Keep a dark period of at least 8 hours, a 24-hour light schedule does not help.
    - heading "What temperature stops chilli plants growing?" [level=3]
    - paragraph: Growth slows sharply below 15 °C at night and stops around 12 °C. Above 30 °C fruit set drops. The comfortable range is 18 to 26 °C.
    - heading "Should I fertilise a chilli plant that is not growing?" [level=3]
    - paragraph: Only if light, temperature and watering are already right and the plant has been in the same compost for more than 6 weeks. Otherwise feeding adds salt stress to a plant that cannot use it.
    - heading "Can I grow chillies in a smart garden all year?" [level=3]
    - paragraph: Yes. A 12-pod hydroponic garden with its own LED gives 14 hours of light and 20 to 24 °C water regardless of the season, which removes causes 1, 2 and 3 at once. Growers commonly report plants reaching 30 to 35 cm within 10 weeks even in winter months.
    - heading "Why is my chilli seedling not growing after germination?" [level=3]
    - paragraph: Seedlings stall for the same reasons as mature plants, but light is usually the culprit. Keep seedlings 10 to 15 cm under a lamp at 16 to 18 °C and move them to their own 9 cm pot once they reach 2 to 3 cm tall.
