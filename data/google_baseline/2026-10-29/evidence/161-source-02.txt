    - time: May 29, 2026

    - paragraph: "Full diagnostic below: how to read the symptoms, what to do for each cause, and the soil fix that breaks the cycle."
    - paragraph: Leaves falling off a succulent is the most common panic-inducing symptom new succulent owners run into. The good news is that it almost always traces back to one of four causes, and the symptoms are easy to tell apart once you know what to look for. The bad news is that the most common cause (overwatering in dense soil) progresses silently for weeks before the first leaf drops, and by the time you see leaf loss the plant is often a step away from total collapse. This guide walks through diagnosis, immediate action for each cause, and the soil change that breaks the cycle.
    - heading "The 4 causes, ranked by frequency" [level=2]
    - heading "1. Overwatering combined with the wrong soil (most common)" [level=3]
    - paragraph: Succulents store water in their leaves. Their roots evolved to absorb moisture in short pulses, then sit dry for days or weeks. When the roots stay wet too long (because the soil holds water too well, or you watered too often, or both), they begin to rot. The rot moves up the stem. As the stem softens, the leaves attached to it lose their grip and drop off, often still plump and green-looking, sometimes black at the base where they detached.
    - paragraph:
      - strong: "Tell-tale signs:"
      - text: leaves drop when touched, the base of the stem looks mushy or black, the soil still feels damp days after watering, you may notice a sour or fermented smell at the soil line, fungus gnats appear near the pot.
    - paragraph:
      - strong: "What to do right now:"
    - list:
      - listitem: Stop watering immediately.
      - listitem: Unpot the plant. Tip the pot sideways and ease the rootball out.
      - listitem: Shake off all the wet soil. Inspect the roots and stem base. Pale, firm roots = healthy. Brown, black, or mushy roots = rot. Cut away any rotten roots with clean scissors. If the stem base is mushy, cut the plant above the rot and let the cut callous over for 2–4 days before replanting the top as a fresh cutting.
      - listitem: Let the bare rootball or cutting air-dry for 24–72 hours.
      - text: Replant in a fast-draining gritty mix like
      - link "Molly's Succulent Mix":
        - /url: /products/mollys-succulent-mix-premium-gritty-soil-mix
      - text: ", in a pot with drainage holes, no saucer of standing water."
      - listitem: Wait a full week before watering again. Then water deeply but only when the mix is bone dry, never on a schedule.
    - heading "2. Underwatering (second most common, less catastrophic)" [level=3]
    - paragraph: The opposite problem. The plant has been dry too long and starts cannibalizing its lowest leaves to keep the top alive. Succulents are good at surviving drought but a long enough drought eventually catches up.
    - paragraph:
      - strong: "Tell-tale signs:"
      - text: leaves shrivel and wrinkle before they drop, the dropped leaves are dry and crispy rather than soft, the soil is bone dry and may have pulled away from the pot edge, the stem looks shrunken or wrinkled.
    - paragraph:
      - strong: "What to do right now:"
    - list:
      - listitem: Water the plant deeply. Soak the soil thoroughly until water runs out the drainage holes.
      - listitem: Let it drain completely. Empty any standing water in the saucer.
      - listitem: Watch the plant for 2–3 days. Plump leaves and improved color = it was thirsty.
      - listitem: From now on, water deeply when the mix is bone dry, typically every 2–3 weeks indoors depending on light and pot size. Always deep watering, never shallow sips.
    - heading "3. Light shock, temperature shock, or relocation" [level=3]
    - paragraph: Succulents handle environmental change less gracefully than tropicals. A sudden move from a low-light corner to a bright window can sunburn leaves and trigger drop. A cold draft from an air conditioner or open window in winter can cold-damage leaves overnight. Even moving from a heated indoor space to an unheated mudroom for a few hours can be enough to make a stressed plant drop.
    - paragraph:
      - strong: "Tell-tale signs:"
      - text: mass leaf drop within days of a move, a repot, or a seasonal change. Often accompanied by leaf scorch marks, soft squishy patches from cold damage, or stretched pale new growth indicating light frustration.
    - paragraph:
      - strong: "What to do right now:"
    - list:
      - listitem: Identify what changed. Move? Repot? Window blocked? AC vent reposition?
      - listitem: "If light: move the plant back closer to its previous condition, then transition gradually over 2 weeks (1 hour more sun per day)."
      - listitem: "If cold: relocate to a stable 18–24°C spot, away from drafts and cold glass. Cold damage often shows up 1–3 days after exposure."
      - listitem: Do not water during the recovery period unless the plant is also dry. Stressed roots cannot absorb water well and standing moisture compounds the damage.
    - heading "4. Natural shedding (don't panic)" [level=3]
    - paragraph: A healthy growing succulent will shed its oldest 1–2 lower leaves periodically as the rosette pushes new growth at the top. This is normal and unrelated to soil, water, or care. The shed leaves are usually evenly aged (older), drop one at a time over weeks not days, and leave behind a normal stem with no rot or damage.
    - paragraph:
      - strong: "Tell-tale signs:"
      - text: 1–2 lower leaves dropping per month, the rest of the plant looks vibrant and is producing new growth, no soft stems, no sudden change.
    - paragraph:
      - strong: "What to do:"
      - text: Nothing. This is your plant being healthy. Pluck the dried leaves off gently if you want to tidy the base.
    - 'heading "Why soil is the upstream fix for cause #1" [level=2]'
    - paragraph: Almost every overwatering case starts with the soil, not the watering can. Standard bagged potting soil is engineered for moisture-loving outdoor annuals. It is peat-heavy, dense, and holds water for days. Put a succulent in that soil and even a "normal" watering schedule (once a week, say) leaves the roots wet long enough to begin rotting. The owner sees a sad-looking plant, decides it needs more water, and accelerates the rot.
    - paragraph: Gritty succulent mix solves this at the source. It is built from chunky inorganic ingredients (pumice, perlite, decomposed granite) plus a small amount of organic material for nutrients. Water rushes through, the roots get a drink, and the soil dries within a day or two. The roots are not left sitting in water, so they get far less time in the wet conditions that lead to rot.

    - heading "How to tell the difference (quick reference)" [level=2]
    - paragraph: "If you are panicking and just want the diagnostic decision tree:"
    - list:
      - strong: Lower stem is mushy or black
      - text: → Cause 1 (overwater + soil rot). Unpot today.
      - strong: Leaves are dry and crispy when they fall
      - text: → Cause 2 (underwater). Water deeply.
      - strong: Leaves are still plump when they fall, no stem rot
      - text: → Likely Cause 3 (shock) or transition period. Stabilize environment.
      - strong: Just 1–2 lower leaves dropping per month, top looks great
      - text: → Cause 4 (normal). Ignore.
      - strong: Sour smell at soil line
      - text: → Cause 1, advanced. Unpot now.
      - strong: Soil pulled away from pot edge
      - text: → Cause 2. Water deeply.
      - strong: Fungus gnats present
      - text: → Cause 1 in progress. Switch soil before it becomes Cause 1 advanced.
    - heading "The prevention recipe" [level=2]
    - paragraph: "Once you get a succulent past the immediate problem, three habits keep it stable indefinitely:"
    - list:
      - strong: The right soil.
      - text: Gritty, fast-draining, soilless.
      - link "Molly's Succulent Mix":
        - /url: /products/mollys-succulent-mix-premium-gritty-soil-mix
      - text: or an equivalent. This single change removes the standing water that most rot cases start from.
      - strong: The right pot.
      - text: Drainage holes are mandatory. Terracotta is ideal because it wicks moisture from the soil through the pot wall, accelerating drying. Avoid pots without drainage and avoid leaving saucers full of water under draining pots.
      - strong: Water deeply, then forget for 2–3 weeks.
      - text: "Succulent care is counterintuitive: most people kill them by being too attentive. Water until water runs out the drainage holes, then completely ignore the plant until the soil is bone dry. Indoors with low light, that may be 3–4 weeks between waterings, not 1."
