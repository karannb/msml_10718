    - paragraph: Brown or black marks on Monstera leaves don’t always mean there’s an issue with the whole plant – it may just be one leaf that’s having trouble with something.
    - paragraph: The main obstacle when it comes to spots on Monstera leaves is diagnosis. It’s NOT easy. Sunburn can look like root rot. Root rot can look like fungus. Over time you can become better at identifying issues, but it’s not an exact science.

    - heading "Root rot" [level=3]
    - paragraph: Root rot is extremely common in plants, usually because we love to overwater them. Other signs of root rot are yellowing leaves.
    - paragraph: Plants don’t need watering nearly as much as we think, and they can get root rot extremely easily.
    - text: The good news here is that Monstera deliciosa are, in my experience, pretty hard to push past the point of no return. I mean if you
    - emphasis: want
    - text: to be rid of one, overwatering is absolutely the way to go, but they can be brought back from it.
    - paragraph: Once the leaves have blackened, browned, or turned yellow, it’s irreversible. The leaf might not drop, but it won’t return to its original green.
    - list:
      - link "How to save an overwatered Monstera":

      - link "How to water Monstera deliciosa properly":
        - /url: https://planethouseplant.com/when-should-you-water-monstera-deliciosa/
    - paragraph: Root rot is caused by bacteria attacking the roots. The roots will go mushy and smell, gross, and will no longer work efficiently. But as I mentioned before, roots can regrow, and pretty quickly in Monstera.
    - paragraph: There’s often a little bit of confusion around putting an overwatered plant in water, but let me explain.
    - paragraph:
      - strong: Plants that are growing in water grow water roots, and plants that are growing in soil grow soil roots.
    - paragraph: They both do the same thing – extract oxygen from their surroundings – but in slightly different ways.
    - generic: A more extreme way to think of it is like how humans can get oxygen by breathing in the air (but will drown in water) and fish are the opposite way around. If you're still confused, check out this post, which explains why waterlogged plants die, yet you can grow a Monstera hydroponically.
    - paragraph: Plants can develop a root system that best suits their surroundings

    - heading "Dehydration/underwatering" [level=3]
    - paragraph: Or, as professional people call it ‘incongruent watering’.
    - paragraph: Monstera like a lot of water, but only when they’re dry.
    - paragraph: Watering once a week is often far too much, especially in colder climates, or if your Monstera is in a low-light situation.
    - paragraph:
      - strong: If you’re unsure, pick up a moisture metre (they’re pretty cheap) and only water when the needle points to 2.
    - paragraph: If you keep watering your plant and the soil is drying out really quickly then try bottom watering your plant.
    - paragraph:
      - strong: Leave it to soak in a couple of inches of water until the top of the soil is damp.
    - paragraph: If you have a big monstera, you can do this in the bath.
    - paragraph: Soil can become hydrophobic, and not absorb any water.
    - generic: Instead, the water runs in the gap between the soil and the plant, and through any cracks in the soil. Giving it a soak rehydrates the soil thoroughly, and helps it hold water again.
    - paragraph: If the soil is still drying out in a couple of days, check that the plant isn’t rootbound.
    - paragraph: The roots will displace the soil so then there’s not enough soil to hold an adequate amount of water.
    - paragraph:
      - strong: If the roots are almost entirely encircling the rootball, it’s time to re-pot.
    - generic: Don't be tempted to dramatically increase the size of the pot, because you'll end up with the opposite problem, and the plant could be at risk from root rot.
    - paragraph: A pot a couple of inches bigger is fine.

    - heading "Sunburn" [level=3]
    - paragraph: Wild Monstera (as it were) live in rainforests, below the canopy. They don’t get a lot of bright, direct light, so if they are exposed to it, then they can get burned.
    - paragraph:
      - strong: This is especially true if you have a variegated Monstera
      - text: – they burn incredibly easily.
    - paragraph: Now the conflict we have here is that in order for your Monstera to grow those big, beautiful fenestrated leaves, it needs A LOT of light.
    - generic: If you're lucky, you have a room filled with bright, indirect light. If you have a south-facing room with great windows, put the Monstera next to window, but hang a sheer curtain. This should stop the plant from burning.
    - paragraph:
      - text: Alternatively, you can gradually increase your plant’s tolerance to the light – this is time-consuming and always carries a
      - link "risk of burning":

      - text: .
    - generic: If you have a warm enough climate your Monstera could grow into a BEAST if you acclimatise it to living outdoors. Put it somewhere where it gets nice light but is shielded from the strongest rays - under a porch or shady tree would be good.

      - strong: You can try grow lights if your Monstera lives in a low-light situation and you don’t want to move it.

    - heading "Cold temperatures" [level=3]
    - paragraph:
      - text: Tropical plants hate the cold.
      - strong: The biggest culprit of black spots caused by the cold is unheated sunrooms or conservatories.
    - paragraph: They remain warm during the day until quite late in the year (especially if they’re south or west-facing) but get really cold at night.
    - generic: "Fun fact: my mum killed a Sansevieria (yes - she's got that much of a black thumb) by leaving it in the conservatory over winter."
    - paragraph:
      - text: I’m not 100% if the plant starts self-destructing as a way of conserving energy –
      - link "like a form going dormant":
        - /url: https://planethouseplant.com/do-house-plants-go-dormant-in-winter/
      - text: – or if the cold weather and immuno-compromised plant attract fungal diseases.
    - paragraph: I think it’s probably a bit of both.
    - text: The problem with glass rooms is that they give the plant a lot of light, so the plant tries to continue growing when it really doesn't have the resources.
    - strong: Either keep the room warm or move your plant to a warmer spot.
    - paragraph: Plants that are kept in a cold spot for a few hours can look FAR worse than mine below. I actually think this spot was caused by watering it with too-cold water when the leaf was still new. It’s just a theory though.

    - heading "Misting" [level=3]
    - paragraph: Plants don’t like getting their leaves wet – it causes the leaves to rot (which will lead to black spots) or it’ll lead to fungal and bacterial diseases (again which will lead to root rot).

    - generic: Monstera aren't massively bothered by misting, since they have pretty thick leaves that won't be easily rotted by standing water BUT it's worth noting that NO ONE (plants, humans, bunnies) enjoys getting rained on.
    - paragraph:
      - strong: SURE it can be a relief if it hasn’t rained in six months BUT in general no one likes getting rained on.
    - paragraph: In an ideal situation, Monstera would live in damp (but not too damp!) soil, and be surrounded by high humidity. Humidity and rain are NOT the same. It’s the difference between sitting in a lovely steamy sauna and having someone pour water on you.

    - paragraph: This isn’t a direct correlation, so low light doesn’t automatically mean that your Monstera will get black spots, but hear me out.
    - paragraph:
      - strong: A Monstera kept in low light is probably going to be a little more stressed than one kept in bright, indirect light.
