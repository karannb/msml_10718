    - paragraph:
      - text: "Start with the simplest divider:"

    - paragraph: One damaged new leaf after delivery, repotting, a dry spell or a heat spike can be temporary. That leaf may already have been forming when the problem happened, so the damage only becomes visible later, once the leaf opens.
    - paragraph: Repeated damage is different. If every new leaf comes out twisted, scarred, tiny, stuck, blackened or misshapen, the cause is probably still active.

    - paragraph: New leaves can show root problems before older leaves collapse.
    - paragraph: Expanding tissue needs a steady water supply. If roots are dry, damaged, suffocated or rotting, new leaves may open smaller, weaker, crumpled or partly stuck.
    - paragraph: Wet substrate is a common trap. A plant can look thirsty while roots are sitting in damp, low-oxygen conditions. Leaves droop, pale growth appears or new leaves deform because damaged roots cannot move water properly, not because the pot needs more water.

    - heading "Fertiliser, Salts and Crispy New Growth" [level=2]
    - paragraph:
      - strong: Fertiliser is not a repair tool for malformed leaves.
    - paragraph: Salt build-up can happen in old substrate, heavily fertilised pots, semi-hydro setups without regular flushing or plants watered with mineral-rich water. Indoors, the risk is higher when light is low, roots are weak or substrate dries unevenly.
    - paragraph:
      - strong: "Salt or fertiliser stress can show as:"

      - listitem: pale weak growth despite feeding
      - listitem: leaf edge burn
      - listitem: white crust on substrate or pot edges
    - paragraph: This does not mean every deformed leaf is a salt problem. It means fertiliser history belongs in the diagnosis.
    - paragraph: If roots are healthy, light is adequate and the plant is actively growing, gentle feeding can support future growth. If roots are damaged, pests are active or substrate is already salty, more fertiliser can push the plant further in the wrong direction.
    - paragraph: 💡 Be careful with one-nutrient fixes. Specific nutrient deficiencies are difficult to diagnose from leaf shape alone indoors.

    - heading "Why New Leaves Get Stuck While Unfurling" [level=2]
    - paragraph: New leaves expand by enlarging soft cells. That process depends on steady water movement, functioning roots and tissue that stays flexible long enough to open cleanly.
    - paragraph:
      - strong: "A leaf can get stuck when:"
    - list:
      - listitem: it catches inside a cataphyll, sheath or rolled growth
      - listitem: watering swings interrupt smooth expansion
      - listitem: roots are too dry to maintain steady water movement
      - listitem: roots are too wet or damaged to supply water properly
      - listitem: heat or strong light increases water demand
      - listitem: very dry local air adds stress around thin unfurling tissue
      - listitem: pests damage soft tissue before the leaf hardens
    - paragraph: Humidity can matter here, but it is not the whole diagnosis. It may support smoother unfurling for some thin-leaved tropical plants, especially when a leaf is already tight inside a sheath. It will not fix thrips, mites, damaged roots, salt build-up, cold damage or substrate that stays wet for too long.
    - paragraph:
      - strong: ❗ Avoid pulling a stuck leaf open.
      - text: Soft tissue can tear easily, and petioles or new leaf surfaces may scar before the leaf has finished expanding.
    - paragraph: 💡 For repeated stuck leaves, check watering rhythm, root health, light, heat, airflow and pest signs. A humidifier or plant cabinet can help when roots are healthy and pests are ruled out.

    - heading "Should You Help a Stuck Leaf Open?" [level=2]
    - paragraph: Usually, no.
    - paragraph: Pulling, peeling cataphylls aggressively or scraping soft tissue with tweezers, nails or tools can tear a leaf that might still open on its own.
    - paragraph:
      - strong: "Safer options:"
    - list:
      - listitem: increase local humidity if the plant is otherwise healthy
      - listitem: move plant away from hot glass, radiators or harsh direct sun
      - listitem: keep watering steady, not erratic
      - listitem: check for pests before touching the new leaf
      - listitem: give the leaf more time
      - listitem: remove only dead, black, soft or foul-smelling tissue
    - paragraph: Damaged parts will not repair. A torn section stays torn. A crumpled section stays crumpled. The goal is not to make the current leaf perfect. The goal is to make the next leaf cleaner.
    - 'heading "Torn New Leaves: Cosmetic Damage or Active Problem?" [level=2]'
    - paragraph: A single tear during unfurling is usually cosmetic. New leaves are soft, folded, rolled or pressed against other tissue before they open. A leaf can catch, split or rub without the whole plant being in decline.
    - paragraph: Old tears do not repair. A torn section may dry along the edge, but the rest of the leaf can still function.
    - paragraph:
      - text: Repeated tearing needs more attention.
      - strong: "Look for a pattern:"
    - list:
      - listitem: leaves keep sticking before they open
      - listitem: new growth dries at the tip
      - listitem: leaves tear in the same place each time
      - listitem: new leaves also show silvering, stippling or black droppings
      - listitem: substrate swings from very dry to very wet
      - listitem: pot stays wet while leaves look weak
      - listitem: plant sits close to hot glass, strong sun or dry moving air
    - paragraph: 💡 If the leaf is mostly green and firm, leave it. Cutting away healthy tissue only reduces the plant’s working surface. Remove only dead, soft, black or badly damaged sections with clean scissors.
    - paragraph: 🔗 If tearing appears together with scars, speckling or rough new growth, move to the pest control guides.

    - heading "Crumpled, Twisted or Distorted New Leaves" [level=2]
    - paragraph: Crumpled new growth matters because it points to stress while the leaf was developing.
    - paragraph:
      - strong: "The most common causes indoors are:"
    - list:
      - listitem: thrips feeding before leaves open
      - listitem: mites feeding on tender new tissue
      - listitem: damaged roots reducing steady water supply
      - listitem: wet substrate reducing oxygen around roots
      - listitem: salt build-up affecting water movement and leaf expansion
      - listitem: unstable watering during active growth
      - listitem: recent repotting or delivery stress
      - listitem: poor light combined with weak roots, slow drying or repeated feeding
    - paragraph:
      - strong: Do not jump straight to fertiliser.
      - text: A deformed leaf is not automatically a hungry leaf. Fertiliser can make things worse if roots are stressed, substrate is salty or light is too low for active growth.
    - paragraph: 💡 One damaged leaf after a dry week, delivery or repotting can be temporary. Several bad new leaves in a row point toward the growing point, pests, roots, substrate or feeding routine.
    - paragraph: 🔗 Use our thrips guide, spider mite guide and root-rot guide to narrow the cause before changing care.
    - heading "New Leaf Problems After Delivery" [level=2]
    - paragraph: A leaf that opens badly after delivery may have started expanding before or during transport.
    - paragraph: Soft new tissue can be marked by darkness, packing pressure, temperature shifts and changes in moisture inside the plant. A new leaf may arrive partly unfurled, open torn a week later, or harden with cosmetic marks that reflect the delivery window rather than current care.
    - paragraph: Monitor before changing the setup.
    - paragraph:
      - strong: "Check:"
    - list:
      - listitem: Is only one new leaf affected?
      - listitem: Are stems and roots firm?
      - listitem: Is substrate drying normally?
      - listitem: Is the next leaf cleaner?
      - listitem: Are there pest signs, black tissue or collapse?
    - paragraph: A rushed repot can add root disturbance on top of transport stress. Wait unless roots, stems or substrate clearly point to a real problem.

    - heading "New Leaf Problems After Repotting" [level=2]
    - paragraph: Repotting disturbs fine roots. New substrate also changes how water moves through the pot.
    - paragraph: A plant may produce one imperfect leaf after repotting because roots are adjusting while the new leaf is expanding. That does not automatically mean the repot failed.
    - paragraph:
      - strong: "Repotting-related new-leaf problems are more likely when:"
    - list:
      - listitem: pot size jumped too far
      - listitem: substrate is much denser than before
      - listitem: substrate is much drier than before
      - listitem: air pockets reduce root contact
      - listitem: roots were heavily trimmed
      - listitem: plant was watered heavily after potting into a slow-drying mix
      - listitem: plant was repotted while already stressed
    - paragraph: Keep care steady. Repeated repotting keeps resetting the root system and can create the instability that causes weak new growth.
    - paragraph: 💡 If problems continue, check root contact, substrate structure, pot size and drying speed.
    - paragraph: 🔗 For a steadier reset, compare your setup with our repotting guide, houseplant substrate guide and drainage vs aeration guide.
    - heading "Is Humidity the Reason New Leaves Are Stuck?" [level=2]
    - paragraph: Sometimes, partly.
    - paragraph: Humidity can help some thin-leaved tropical and collector plants unfold new growth more smoothly, especially when roots are healthy and pests are not present. It can reduce dry-air stress around soft tissue while a new leaf is expanding.

    - list:
      - listitem: Avoid pulling stuck leaves open.
      - listitem: Do not mist as a treatment for every stuck leaf.
      - listitem: Do not fertilise repeatedly to force better leaves.

    - heading "Plant-Specific New-Leaf Notes" [level=2]
    - heading "Anthurium" [level=3]
    - paragraph: Anthurium leaves can stay soft for a long time while they expand and harden. Minor tears or dents may be cosmetic, especially after shipping or a dry spell. Repeated distorted leaves need checks for thrips, mites, root health and salt build-up. Stable humidity can support smoother expansion, but it does not fix active pests or failing roots.
    - heading "Philodendron" [level=3]
    - paragraph: Many climbing Philodendron species push leaves through cataphylls, where soft tissue can catch, rub or tear. Self-heading types can also produce distorted new leaves when roots are stressed or pests feed on young growth. Check inside tight cataphylls if scars are already present when the leaf appears, and avoid pulling leaves free by hand.
    - heading "Monstera" [level=3]
    - paragraph: Monstera splits and holes are normal mature leaf development when they match the plant’s fenestration pattern. Ragged tears, silver scars, crumpled tissue or distorted new leaves are different. Those signs point more toward pests, root stress, heat, shipping pressure or mechanical damage during unfurling.
    - heading "Alocasia" [level=3]
    - paragraph: Alocasia can produce imperfect new leaves after delivery, repotting, cold stress or root disturbance. Repeated deformed leaves, wet substrate, soft corm tissue or collapsing petioles need a root-zone check. Treat humidity as support only after roots, temperature and pests make sense.
    - heading "Syngonium" [level=3]
    - paragraph: Syngonium leaves change shape as the plant matures, especially when allowed to climb. Smaller or simpler leaves can follow low light or unsupported growth over time. Twisted, scarred or repeatedly malformed new leaves still need pest, root and watering checks first.
    - heading "Hoya" [level=3]
    - paragraph: Hoya new growth can stall, dry back or deform when roots are unstable, light or heat is too harsh, or pests are present. Soft leaves plus repeated watering should trigger a root check. A leaner, airier mix often makes more sense than extra feeding when the pot stays wet for too long.
    - heading "Prayer Plants" [level=3]
    - paragraph: Rolled new leaves are normal during emergence, but leaves that harden twisted, torn, stippled or bronzed need moisture, salt and pest checks. Spider mites are especially worth ruling out on leaf undersides, where early damage can be easy to miss.
    - heading "Ficus" [level=3]
    - paragraph: Ficus new growth may pause, emerge smaller or harden imperfectly after a move, dry spell or root disturbance. Repeated distorted shoots, sticky residue or clustered pests point toward sap-feeding pests such as scale or mealybugs.
    - 'heading "📌 Final Checklist: How to Read New-Leaf Problems" [level=2]'
    - list:
      - listitem: Check whether it is one bad leaf or repeated damage.
      - listitem: Inspect newest growth, undersides and growing points for pests.
      - listitem: Isolate the plant if pest signs appear on new leaves.
      - listitem: Check whether substrate is staying too wet or drying too hard.
      - listitem: Look at roots if wet substrate and weak growth appear together.
      - listitem: Review fertiliser and salt history.
      - listitem: Think back to delivery, repotting, heat, cold or a dry spell.
      - listitem: Use humidity as support only after pests and roots make sense.
      - listitem: Judge progress by the next leaf.
    - paragraph: 💡 One imperfect new leaf is usually not a disaster. Repeated distorted new growth is a signal. Read it from newest tissue, root zone and recent plant history, not from humidity alone.
    - heading "FAQ" [level=2]
    - heading "Why is my houseplant’s new leaf stuck?" [level=3]
    - paragraph: A new leaf can get stuck when soft tissue catches inside a sheath, cataphyll or rolled growth. Unstable watering, dry air around the growing point, root stress, heat and pests can all make unfurling less smooth.
    - heading "Should I help a stuck leaf open?" [level=3]
    - paragraph: Do not pull it open. Soft tissue tears easily. Stabilise watering, check pests, improve local humidity if the plant is otherwise healthy and give the leaf time.
    - heading "Why did my new leaf tear while unfurling?" [level=3]
    - paragraph: A single tear can happen mechanically while a leaf is still soft and folded. Repeated tearing points more toward unstable moisture, sticking, pests, root stress or harsh drying conditions.
    - heading "Why are new leaves coming out deformed?" [level=3]
    - paragraph: Repeated deformed new leaves usually mean the plant is dealing with an active problem while leaves are forming. Check thrips, mites, root health, substrate moisture, salt build-up, light and recent repotting or delivery stress.
    - heading "Do thrips cause deformed new leaves?" [level=3]
    - paragraph: Yes. Thrips can feed on developing tissue before a leaf fully opens. New leaves may unfurl twisted, scarred, silvery or marked with small black droppings.
    - heading "Can spider mites damage new leaves?" [level=3]
    - paragraph: Yes. Spider mites can cause stippling, dullness, webbing and weak growth. Broad mites and related mites can also cause bronzing, curling, twisting and distorted young tissue.
    - heading "Is low humidity causing stuck leaves?" [level=3]
    - paragraph: It can be one factor, especially for thin-leaved tropical plants. But humidity is not a cure for pests, root damage, salt build-up, cold damage or poor light combined with weak growth.
    - heading "Why did my plant grow a deformed leaf after shipping?" [level=3]
    - paragraph: The leaf may have started expanding before or during shipping. Darkness, packing pressure, temperature shifts and changes in moisture inside the plant can affect soft tissue. Watch the next leaf before assuming the problem is ongoing.
    - heading "Why are new leaves deformed after repotting?" [level=3]
    - paragraph: Repotting disturbs fine roots and changes moisture rhythm. One imperfect leaf can happen while roots adjust. Repeated problems need a check of pot size, substrate structure, root contact and drying speed.
    - heading "Will a torn new leaf heal?" [level=3]
    - paragraph: No. Torn leaf tissue does not knit back together. The leaf may continue working if enough green surface remains, but the tear itself stays visible.
    - heading "Should I cut off a deformed new leaf?" [level=3]
    - paragraph: Only if it is mostly dead, soft, black, infected-looking or blocking growth. If it is green and firm, it can still support the plant.
